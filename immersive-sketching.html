Immersive Sketching
From Photogrammetry
to Augmented Design

Two Digitization Campaigns

We conducted two 3D digitization campaigns in the gardens of the château, the first in July and the second in September 2022. In addition to these primary missions, we carried out spontaneous scans in and around Paris as specific needs emerged throughout the project.

During the first campaign, we were able to capture gardens with a greater abundance of flowers than in September. However, the primary challenge we encountered was the heat. Capturing tens of thousands of photographs under high temperatures repeatedly caused our equipment to malfunction. Despite these conditions, we completed approximately fifty scans in total at the Domaine de Chaumont-sur-Loire.

We utilized PhotoCatch to generate 3D models from the collected photographs. Each image corresponds to a flower, generated by photogrammetric algorithms that translate 2D images into 3D models.

Optimization of 3D Models

The models produced through photogrammetric scanning are refined and cleaned so that they appear as fragments of reality, isolated and shaped into virtual clones. Their geometries and textures are further optimized through compression algorithms to facilitate efficient visualization within augmented reality environments.

Botanical Classification

The next stage involved the creation of a catalog of the digitized gardens, organized according to their scales and the botanical classification of the flowers they contain. Through this process, we identified shared characteristics among the different gardens and grouped them according to botanical families:

  • Asteraceae
  • Herbaceous plants
  • Ericales
  • Succulents
  • Inflorescences

Asteraceae

Commonly known as the family of asters, daisies, composites, or sunflowers, most species within Asteraceae are herbaceous plants, whether annual, biennial, or perennial. Their defining characteristic lies in the presence of numerous small individual florets grouped together and held by protective involucral bracts within a capitulum. This family has a remarkably wide distribution, found on all continents except Antarctica.

Inflorescence

An inflorescence is a cluster or grouping of flowers arranged along a stem, composed either of a main axis or a more complex branching structure. The flowers within an inflorescence develop according to specific reproductive patterns, allowing for lateral or central growth. Their bracts serve both to attract pollinators and to protect developing flowers.

Conventional 3D Recomposition

We produced a series of 3D collages by recomposing digitized fragments drawn from heterogeneous botanical realities. These assemblages are then deployed as immersive environments to be explored through augmented reality.

At this stage, we examined how these botanical scans, treated as spatial components, could enter into a design process more familiar to the architect, specifically one mediated through the computer. We developed a working method in which the intermediate phase of drawing takes place digitally, situated between the initial digitization and the final immersive experience.

These necessary articulations led us away from BIM environments such as Autodesk Revit, which tend to align architecture with the domains of engineering, commerce, and standardization. Such platforms remain distant from the technical, theoretical, and artistic flexibility required for the discipline to renew itself in the age of artificial intelligence.

Our 3D collages, conceived as recycled virtual gardens, are produced using Cinema 4D, a tool widely employed in the digital arts. Once constructed, each garden is visualized and tested in augmented reality. Based on these tests, the model is subsequently modified on the computer, allowing for an iterative exchange between Cinema 4D and augmented reality that can be repeated as many times as necessary.

Immersive Recomposition

Following these initial experiments, we explored how augmented reality could be used directly within the act of design itself. Our aim was to set aside the computer during the design phase and to compose our "garden spolia" in an immersive, spatial, and immediate manner using an iPad or iPhone.

One of the key concepts that emerged from this research and development process is that of the immersive sketch. While three-dimensional environments designed entirely in augmented reality can be highly effective and visually compelling, they still require computer-based refinement to achieve greater precision. These models do not constitute final outcomes; rather, they serve as initial spatial sketches that facilitate subsequent stages of design.

Augmented reality thus appears to offer a significant transformation of the traditional architectural sketch. These intuitive and rapidly produced drawings are no longer confined to the flat surface of paper, but instead unfold spatially, surrounding the designer and expanding the field of architectural imagination.

At this stage, we utilized Reality Composer, the application developed by Apple for the design of immersive experiences. This tool allows us to import our components and reassemble them directly in augmented reality. Beyond standard operations such as scaling and rotation, it also enables animation, the addition of interactive behaviors, and the integration of sound.

Once the composition is established, it is imported into Cinema 4D, where it serves as a reference for the development of a more precise final model. This model is then visualized in augmented reality for testing, and, if necessary, further modifications are carried out in Cinema 4D.

In conclusion, augmented reality does not eliminate the role of the computer in the design process, but rather enriches it through the introduction of the immersive sketch. It establishes a dynamic back-and-forth between computer and tablet, creating a vital alternation between two-dimensional representation and spatial, embodied design.
