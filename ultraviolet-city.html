Philosophy of Plant Life

The aim of this research is to examine and outline the foundations of a possible alliance between the botanical and the digital, while envisioning the potential such an alliance may hold for the design of future urban habitats.

As evidenced by the growing body of scientific literature on artificial life, neurobiology, and plant intelligence, we are currently re-examining and redefining fundamental concepts such as "intelligence" and "life." This research seeks to engage with and expand these contemporary currents of thought, including posthuman critique. This perspective integrates multiple forms of intelligence within a single, inclusive planetary ecology that encompasses vegetal, animal, human, and artificial forms.

The project also aims to deepen reflection on the development of new approaches of architectural and urban design. These approaches move beyond anthropocentric frameworks, instead relying on the balanced collaboration of artificial, plant, and animal forms of life.

Our research in the field of botany focuses on the systems of communication, intelligence, and interspecies collaboration that often remain invisible and elusive. Through an experimental mapping of plant habitats as perceived within the entomological world, we aim to render these microscopic, subterranean, and airborne networks more tangible. Our goal is to highlight the reciprocity between species coexisting within a shared ecology.

What does it feel like to be a peony flower?

How does a bee perceive the world?

Addressing such questions requires, first, a study of the unique sensory capacities of specific plants and insects, their modes of existence, and the ways in which they experience space and time.

Speculative Vegetation

To facilitate the weaving of connections between urban space, the digital realm, and the vegetal world, we studied selected works of science fiction where plant life is fundamental to imagining the cities of the future, whether fantastical, utopian, or dystopian.

Within these works, authors envision new types of spaces where human and vegetal life converge. These narratives challenge the conventional divide between human and non-human worlds, questioning traditional taxonomic separations. As critiques of anthropocentric attitudes and certain transhumanist aspirations, these stories propose, for instance:

  • "Flower-cities" constructed from vegetal matter as the final refuge of civilization in a post-apocalyptic world;
  • Communication systems based entirely on pollen and pheromones;
  • Aromas capable of bending space-time and nectars that weave connections between distant cities;
  • Living houses that grow from seeds like flowers.

While technophile scenarios imagine the future city dweller as a cyborg, part human and part machine, these narratives of floral renaissance conceive of a chimeric being: half human and half plant.

These literary works serve as a vital source of inspiration for developing compelling urban scenarios. We also examine the representation of imaginary vegetal environments in science fiction cinema, seeking visual references to inform and enrich these explorations.

The Mystery of the Third Planet (1982) explores variations of scale and color that resonate with the visual strategies later used in Avatar to construct fantastical, extraterrestrial vegetal environments.
The vegetal cities and imaginary flora of the planet Pandora in Avatar (2009) feature bioluminescent plants that evoke ultraviolet photography of flowers. Their exaggerated proportions generate landscapes that appear both immersive and radically alien.

Neo-Assyrian representations of terraced gardens, canals, aqueducts, and monuments. Drawings traced from bas-reliefs of wall panels, Nineveh (Iraq), circa 7th century BCE.

Hanging Gardens of Babylon

The legendary accounts of the Hanging Gardens of Babylon represent a historical and utopian model for our experimental conception of a fusion between the contemporary city and nature in the age of artificial intelligence. These narratives convey a timeless dream held since the earliest cities: the aspiration to merge the urban environment with the natural world.

These gardens embody a dual urban symbolism that is both nostalgic and forward-looking. On one hand, they evoke the traces of our original habitat, reflecting the condition in which humans lived as hunter-gatherers within nature itself. This period gave rise to myths of the Garden of Eden, the tradition of Persian gardens, and broader imaginaries of a floral Paradise.

On the other hand, these gardens represent the technological capacity of urban society to shape its natural and cultural environments by materializing its urban reveries. Complex irrigation systems, large-scale water-lifting mechanisms, extensive networks of canals and aqueducts, and advanced construction techniques such as fired brick all contributed to this achievement. In this way, the Babylonians realized the dream of a garden-city through the constructive and urban possibilities afforded by their technologies.

These narratives provide a design model in which technology, nature, and the city are inextricably intertwined. Our aim is to reactivate this ancient vision through contemporary digital tools, replacing Babylonian hydraulic networks with networks of Wi-Fi, algorithms, and pixels to revive a holistic urban vision.

To rethink the Hanging Gardens of Babylon in the age of artificial intelligence, to adapt them to contemporary cities, and to weave a planetary network of suspended gardens.

Through the Eyes of a Bee

Artistic reference: an immersive online experience designed by Marshmallow Laser Feast, based on the 3D scanning of a forest environment as part of their project In the Eyes of the Animal. The primary work consisted of a virtual reality experience set within an English forest, where a VR headset allowed participants to perceive the environment through the unique sensory worlds of different animals.

The accompanying online platform and the animated film released on YouTube extended the project's reach, ensuring the work could be shared more widely and preserved beyond the duration of the initial physical installation.

Images of a floral bouquet within the human-visible spectrum, captured using a mechano-optical device that closely approximates the spatial resolution of bee vision. The first image shows a segment that could easily be mistaken for fragmented parts of a scene, while the second reveals how a holistic view discloses the true form and structure of the flowers. The third suggests the potential completion and processing of the image within the brain.

While humans readily perceive the Gestalt of a scene, it was long assumed that insects such as bees relied only on elementary visual cues. Recent research, however, demonstrates that bees favor global processing while retaining the ability to attend to local elements within complex visual environments.

A.G. Dyer and S.K. Williams, "Mechano-optical lens array to simulate insect vision photographically," Imaging Science Journal, vol. 53, 2005, pp. 209–213.

Ultraviolet Macro Photography

One of our primary objectives during the digitization phase is to reveal the colors and forms of the fantastic worlds that coexist alongside our own. Through this lens, one might perceive the world from the perspective of an ant climbing the peduncle of a jasmine flower, or a bee gathering pollen from the stamens of a hibiscus. A blue petal that appears relatively uniform to human vision may thus unfold into a rolling landscape—an extraterrestrial topography marked by hundreds of tonal variations.

To this end, we combine macro photography with ultraviolet imaging to construct three-dimensional environments that simulate the sensory worlds of insects. At present, there are no existing examples of 3D digitization that integrate these two distinct approaches.

Through the use of specialized lenses, macro photography enables a dramatic shift in scale, bringing us closer to the perceptual viewpoint of the insect. Textures, patterns, reliefs, and color gradients that previously remained unseen begin to delineate a floral architecture and organic landscapes that are at once familiar and foreign, appearing as if they emerged from a fantastical or extraterrestrial world.

Ultraviolet photography further enhances the perceptual capacity of the human eye by utilizing UV illumination to access colors beyond the visible spectrum. Unlike humans, most insects are capable of perceiving ultraviolet light. Under these conditions, nectar acquires a fluorescent appearance, tracing patterns across petals that remain invisible to human sight and appearing as luminous landing guides for pollinating insects.
